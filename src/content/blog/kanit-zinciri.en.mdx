Saying "scrap increased by 12 percent" is easy. The harder part is making sure everyone can answer the same questions.

Which period? Which products? Which machines? Which records produced the calculation? What changed later?

A finding becomes defensible through the evidence chain behind the number.

## Five links in the evidence chain

1. **Source record:** which system and record produced the result?
2. **Metric definition:** how was the measure calculated, filtered and bounded in time?
3. **Entity context:** which customer, product, machine, order, supplier or account is related?
4. **Change history:** when did the change begin and has the pattern appeared before?
5. **Decision record:** what was done, who approved it and what happened next?

If the evidence chain stops at the decision, the company remembers what it did but loses why it did it and whether it worked.

## Why evidence belongs next to the finding

A decision-maker should be able to inspect the source, period, related entities and calculation behind a result.

This reduces persuasive-looking results with unclear grounding and repeated analyst-to-management loops asking where a number came from.

## Where the language model belongs

A language model can help explain an investigation, summarize context and understand follow-up questions. It should not be the authority for numeric results.

Numbers come from the defined analytical layer. The model can explain them and propose the next investigation step.

That separation is fundamental if Dima is to be an auditable decision system rather than merely a persuasive conversational tool.
